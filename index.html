You're not just watching. You're in the game.

Other review platforms make the teacher a spectator. You launch the game and then… watch. Raid Review makes you part of the fight — and your students know it.

Your job isn't just to push questions. It's to read the room. Are students communicating? Calling out targets? Encouraging each other? A Medic just healed a teammate and someone yelled "thanks for the heal!" — that's the moment. Hit Rally and reward them with +25% ult charge across the board. When I play with my class, I ask: "Are we ready for a rally?!" — and the room erupts.

Maybe the team is clicking — communication is strong, everyone's in sync. Drop an Orbital Strike and blast 10,000 damage straight into the boss. That's your way of saying I see you working together, here's a reward.

Want to raise the stakes? Flip on Chaos Mode and become the villain. Meteor Strikes rain down, shields get drained, and suddenly students are screaming "NOOOOO!" while scrambling to survive. You control the pressure. Maybe you want to prove a point — you're not working as a team and communicating effectively — and Chaos Mode makes that lesson hit.

But here's where it gets real. If students are arguing, blaming each other for wrong answers, not communicating — that's a teachable moment. You can pause and talk about it. Or you can turn up the pressure and let them feel what happens when a team falls apart. The game creates the moment. You decide what to do with it.

This is what separates Raid Review from everything else. It doesn't just review content. It teaches students how to communicate, strategize, and work as a team. And you're not on the sidelines — you're the one making it happen.

No dev team. No funding.
Just a teacher who built it.

Raid Review wasn't built in a startup office. It was built between grading papers and lesson planning — by a classroom teacher who got tired of watching students silently tap screens during review games and decided to build something better.

Every line of code, every animation, every game mechanic was vibe-coded by one person. I had no coding background. No CS degree. I taught myself how to do all of this — and I put in more hours than I can count learning from mistakes, breaking things, rebuilding them, and figuring out why the screen was blank at 1 AM. It's been quite the journey.

I built the game I wished existed for my classroom. And I hope this encourages other teachers, educators, and students to do the same thing — build the education platform you always wanted. If a 7th grade teacher can learn to ship a game from scratch, the barrier is lower than you think.

This platform costs real money to run, and right now I'm covering it myself. It's free for teachers because teachers shouldn't have to pay for tools that make their classrooms better. If this grows into something bigger, the core game stays free — that's a promise from someone who's been on your side of the projector for two decades.

If Raid Review earns a spot in your classroom, tell another teacher. That's all I ask.
